The Epic of Gilgamesh is a story about the king of Uruk, who befriends Enkidu and together they face several tribulations. After Enkidu dies, he is frightened of death and seeks eternal life. This quest is not successful and he returns home empty handed.

This epic provides a glimpse into cultural values and practices at that time, like the relationship to the gods or the roles of women. The following citations are taken from the translation by Maureen Gallery Kovacs available on the [Uruk Warka website](https://uruk-warka.dk/Gilgamish/The%20Epic%20of%20Gilgamesh.pdf)

As in most ancient narratives, the gods play a central role in the story. There is an ambivalence in the portrayal of the Mesopotamian gods. On the one hand, gods are often portrayed as showing few regards to the life of humans and being primarily concerned with themselves. For example, the god Ea speaks to Enlil after a flood that has wiped out most of humanity: "how could you bring about a Flood without consideration." (p.47) or Ishtar threatening to open the gates of the Netherworld such that the dead go up to eat the living in order to get her way (see p.23). On the other hand, they are also capable of showing compassion and goodwill toward the people. For example, Ea urges Utanapishtim to build a boat and "keep alive living beings!" in order for them to survive the flood (p.43). Also, the goddess Ishtar, among others, weeps for the fate of humans during the flood: "the gods humbly sat weeping, sobbing with grief" (p.46). Therefore, while gods are capable of compassion, the previous discussion highlights that Mesopotamians likely did not believe that the gods could be relied on for protection and good fortune.

In addition to the difficult relationship with their gods, Mesopotamians also seemed to struggle with life and what followed, given the sentiment that is present in this epic. Gilgamesh for example says to Enkidu: "As for human beings, their days are numbered, and whatever they keep trying to achieve is but wind!" (p.10). It conveys the message that death is certain, which underlies the epic in general: Gilgamesh traversed the mountains, crossed the sea, and faced the beasts of wilderness (see p.41) in order to find the secret of eternal life. Utanapishtim, however, reminds him of the futility of his quest: "You have toiled without cease, and what have you got! Through toil you wear yourself out, you fill your body with grief, your long lifetime you are bringing near" (p.42). Even his final attempt to return to a state of youth is a failure (see p.50). Progression of time and death is portrayed as inevitable. But when paired with how the afterlife is portrayed, life suddenly becomes more meaningful: "[in the House of Darkness] where those who dwell do without light, where dirt is their drink, their food is of clay [...] everywhere I listened, it was the bearers of crowns, who, in the past, had ruled the land but who now served Anu and Enlil" (p.28f). Thus despite the struggles of life, it is portrayed as more bearable than what follows.
