# Does the Code of Hammurabi Support the Claim That Ancient Babylonian Society was a Patriarchy?
The Code of Hammurabi is a legal text which contains the judgments spoken by King Hammurabi of Babylon, which were then written down. It concerns criminal offences like stealing and violence but also covers family issues like marriage, as well as economic issues like malpractice and pricing.

The following citations are taken from the translation by L. W. King available on the [Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamframe.asp).

Before considering the actual text, one must consider the definition of patriarchy. The [Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/patriarchy) defines it as follows: "[patriarchy is] a society in which the oldest male is the leader of the family, or a society controlled by men in which they use their power to their own advantage".

There are several judgments in the code that suggest that the ancient Babylonian society was one in which the oldest male was the leader of the family. First, most laws within the family section (from 127. onward) begin with "If a man ...". This shows that the action of the man, not that of the other family members is of importance. Furthermore, the practice of filial piety also supports the argument of a patriarchy. For example, law 195 states that "if a son strike his father, his hands shall be hewn off". Thus, the son must pay respect towards his father and under no circumstance may disrespect him. If he does, he can even be disowned by him (see law 169). The children stand in such close association with their father, that they even serve as collateral: the wife and children can be sold into into forced labor if the father goes into debt (see law 117) and if a man fatally strikes a pregnant women, his daughter must die in return (see law 210). These considerations establish the father as the most powerful individual in the family. 

In addition, several laws also indicate that a system exists that contributes to their advantage. First, women were likely bound to the household with few attainable professions mentioned. For example, laws 141 and 143 both portray negligence of her household as an offence, suggesting that this is an important duty of a wife, and the only occupations mentioned to be filled by a woman were tavern-keeper and sister of god (see laws 108 and 110). These circumstances significantly limit the opportunities for women to make a living for themselves to accumulate wealth and power. Laws 133 to 135 even cover the case in which there is not enough sustenance at home when the husband cannot provide for the family. 

In summary, the code of Hammurabi supports the argument that ancient Mesopotamian society was a patriarchy. All family members must not disrespect the man of the household and he has significant power over them. Since women were largely excluded from participating in society and largely unable to accumulate wealth themselves, man mostly filled out the most powerful and prestigious positions.
